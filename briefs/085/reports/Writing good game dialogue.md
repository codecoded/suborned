# Break the one cadence every speaker shares

Good game dialogue is someone wanting something now, against someone in the way, with the useful fact escaping as a move in that contest and a plain carrier confirming it afterwards. The samples in [scenes.json](/Users/sky/Projects/suborned/briefs/085/fable/scenes.json) already have the right size, at 73 words a scene against a median of 74 in a counted set of Hades conversations, and they have shed the older faults such as greetings and named feelings. What remains is sameness and transaction: **36 of 50 lines are exactly 9 or 10 words, a frightened waitress and a professional fixer speak in the same rhythm (mean 8.9 and 9.1 words, sd 1.1 for both), all 8 lines ending in a question get an on-topic reply on the very next line, and no line ever breaks off.** Much of this is requested by our own inputs: "aim for 10", a 10-word limit in Fable's system prompt, sheets whose phrases reappear as speech, and an editor told to prize strong lines. Shipped games show a way out, because the talk can stay loose when a marker and a plain restatement carry the fact. The first fixes are cheap: human-written voice samples, per-speaker speech rules, sheets stripped to bare facts, and a mechanical rhythm check. These samples were hand-written by Fable, so the pipeline itself has never been judged, and whether rhythm is what Sky hears as "bad" is untested. Sky has eight decisions to make, listed near the end, chiefly whether "clearly explain" binds the spoken lines or the whole scene, and whether a human writes the voices.

## Good lines are moves in a contest, and the fact comes out sideways

The screen and stage sources agree on the base rule. Sorkin says "intention and obstacle is everything" ([No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/2016/07/aaron-sorkin-ama)). Mamet asks "WHO WANTS WHAT? WHAT HAPPENS IF [THEY] DON'T GET IT? WHY NOW?" ([memo copy](https://gist.github.com/jm3/7882146)), and requires a scene to end with someone thwarted or shown another way ([SlashFilm](https://www.slashfilm.com/a-letter-from-david-mamet-to-the-writers-of-the-unit/)). Josh Sawyer gives the sharpest test for a game: "why is this character the character to tell the story?", and "if the answer is 'well, we need someone to tell it,' that's not a good answer" ([Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/pentiment-obsidian-josh-sawyer-interview-xbox-gamepass-1849932260)). Milena fails that test today. Mamet's rule that two people discussing a third is a "crock" ([SlashFilm](https://www.slashfilm.com/a-letter-from-david-mamet-to-the-writers-of-the-unit/)) cannot be obeyed literally in a Listen scene, because overhearing is built on it. It can be honoured by making the third person a weapon between the two talkers.

At the level of the line, the most usable advice comes from a teacher and one game writer. David Freeman's list names the A-B fault ("How are you?" / "Good.") and its remedies: answer with a question, follow your own track, delay the answer, imply it, interrupt, and give bit players a personality in one line ([Destructoid](https://destructoid.com/?p=22071)). Jon Ingold builds a line from three moves, accept, reject or deflect, and his bad example is a scene of pure information exchange ([Radiator Blog](https://www.blog.radiator.debacle.us/2018/11/notes-on-sparking-dialogue-great.html); Yang's interpretation, not a transcript). The 8-of-8 count is Freeman's fault measured. One caution from the notes: Freeman also recommends fragments, and fragments given to everybody produce exactly our monotony, so the variation has to follow the person's want.

Voice is the thinnest sourced area. The solid practice is reading aloud, with someone other than the writer performing the lines ([Obsidian, Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2012-10-09-how-to-write-fallout-new-vegas-dlc.html)). Guy Hasson, a blogger with less authority, warns against using length as a character shortcut and against repeating names ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/story-design-tips-10-dialogue-don-ts)). A one-page voice sheet is the notes' own synthesis, not a sourced method: for each named person, what they notice first, what they refuse to say, what they talk about instead, and how they answer a question. Inez notices who can see; Zhou notices price and exposure; Okafor notices anything out of routine, which is why his best line works. The Fixer comes first, because he has the most lines and only a function. Sean Vanaman's point that writers endear people through humour and mutual respect ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/writing-i-firewatch-i-and-capturing-the-beauty-of-being-alone)) and Ingold's reading of Blade Runner, where the cop loses ([Kotaku, search extract only](https://kotaku.com/1830798679)), both suggest letting him lose or pay for something.

Bubbles make this harsher than the sources assume. Their examples lean on performers, and bubbles have no voice. Subtext here needs a visible clash between the line and something the player already knows, so the fact must be planted first, in the scene or just before it. A cold player will read "Nothing's wrong" flat unless that holds.

### Clarity against subtlety: the sources pull apart, and Sky's instruction settles the game

The notes disagree openly. Wilder via Lubitsch says "Let the audience add up two plus two" ([Open Culture](https://openculture.com/2013/08/10-tips-from-billy-wilder-on-how-to-write-a-good-screenplay.html)). Pinter calls speech a "smoke screen" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristics_of_Harold_Pinter%27s_work)). Ingold says that if the meaningful interaction is in the subtext, "the text itself doesn't matter that much" ([Kotaku, search extract only](https://kotaku.com/1830798679)). Against them stand [writer.md](/Users/sky/Projects/suborned/briefs/085/fable/writer.md) ("Clarity beats subtlety"), Sky's "clearly explain", and Robert Yang's warning that "gamers have been trained specifically to ignore subtext" ([Radiator Blog](https://www.blog.radiator.debacle.us/2018/11/notes-on-sparking-dialogue-great.html)). Disco Elysium reportedly repeats critical information through its skills, though that is a third-hand forum summary ([RPG Codex](https://rpgcodex.net/article.php?id=11309)).

My position, following the craft note, splits the job. **Say plainly what is asked, where, when and at what price. Keep how people feel about it oblique.** Negotiation resolves the conflict by itself, since it is the one situation where people really do spell things out: Zhou's "Spell it out" forces "Service bay, round the back, east yard", and there the plain statement is the fight. Three carriers can then hold the clarity burden: the Scout's closing line, the `learned` and `opens` cards, and a negotiation line. That frees the other lines from confessing. This is a synthesis, and no writer states it as a rule. It also depends on a call only Sky can make, because he asked for the cutscene itself to explain the conversation.

## Shipped games let the talk stay loose because another layer restates the fact

Eavesdropping as a route to new actions is well established. Most Hitman Opportunities begin with 47 overhearing sensitive talk, and players can choose among three guidance levels, from off to waypoints on every step ([Game Critics](https://gamecritics.com/corey-motley/hitmans-golden-opportunities/)). Desperados III, the closest match to our camera, lets you listen in on civilians "to get hints on new gameplay options" ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/designing-the-real-time-stealth-and-combat-of-i-desperados-iii-i-)). Its Mission 10 marks talkers with question marks, splits one clue across six conversations, and lets a player who skips them check more suspects instead ([GamePressure](https://www.gamepressure.com/desperados-iii/get-a-disguise-and-eavesdrop-on-convo/z0e335)). Listening is a shortcut there, which suits "more than one way". Thief's guards talk about boots, ale and mutton ([Wikiquote](https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Thief_(series))), and Kotaku calls one Hitman background man's calls about scones and an overdue rental "unusually well-written" ([Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/the-hidden-life-of-a-random-hitman-npc-1773218201)).

**The pattern across these is three layers: a marker on the talkers, loose talk, then a plain restatement outside the fiction.** That is the note's reading across games, not any studio's stated rule. `listen-inez` already has the layers, as the talk, the Scout, then the cards, so the talk need not carry the fact alone. Keep the jobs apart: the Scout says what is true, and the option says what you could do, at its price. Game Critics' warning applies, since a chain of step prompts "shows players exactly how" and would read as one right route. A marker on the talker at the moment the fact lands is the note's proposal, with no shipped example beyond Desperados' pre-talk marks.

Valve's system is the best-documented machinery, and all of it can be built ahead of time. Criteria go in, the rule with the most matching criteria wins, general lines remain as fallbacks, and rules write memory back. A conversation is a chain of lookups, so it self-terminates when its conditions stop being true, and long speeches are cut into pieces so they can break cleanly. Ruskin notes that spoken state is "an important prompt to players about actions they may need to take" ([Ruskin, GDC 2012](https://cdn.fastly.steamstatic.com/apps/valve/2012/GDC2012_Ruskin_Elan_DynamicDialog.pdf)). This fits the no-runtime-AI rule, provided any random gate draws from the run's seed so that a saved run still replays. Skyrim shows the difference specificity makes: most guard lines need a condition, such as the Thieves Guild armour line, while the arrow-in-the-knee line has requirement "None" ([UESP](https://en.uesp.net/wiki/Skyrim:Guard_Dialogue)). That line and New Vegas's "Patrolling the Mojave" became memes through repetition ([Know Your Meme](https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/patrolling-the-mojave)). A generated line should name something the player can check in that run. Brandon Hennessy's account of Watch Dogs: Legion is the warning for generated people: writing for characters you can't know has "all of the narrative energy of a tug of war where neither side is fully holding the rope", and "a single authored character" makes a procedural sea cohere ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/gdc2023/how-i-watch-dogs-legion-i-made-ubisoft-rethink-its-storytelling)).

Comedy helps and hurts, and the evidence is one-sided. Critics credit mundane worries with making background people feel real. The harm is the note's analysis only, with nothing measuring it: a bubble asks to be read, so idle chatter competes with the clue in a way spoken chatter does not. The position worth taking is that one line should do both jobs, as "He crosses the whole square for one coffee" tries to. Keep comedy out of the Scout and the options, and treat jokes at Inez's expense as a tone decision.

No source addresses a player arriving partway through a conversation. The note's proposal tags each beat with its fact and awards only what was heard. That problem exists only if the talk plays in the street. In a stopped-clock cutaway it disappears, which makes it part of the format decision. The evidence here has real holes: **no verified Hitman Mission Story lines, no verified Dishonored or Watch Dogs lines, and no published durations for any overheard scene.** Those pages refused automated fetches, and nothing was filled in from memory.

## Scene size is right; bubble timing and idle staging are the weak parts

Sixty-eight to 77 words a scene matches Hades, whose counted conversations have a median of 74 words and 3 turns, so 78% have four turns or fewer ([Nyx transcript and others](https://hades.fandom.com/wiki/Nyx/Quotes_(Hades))). The samples spend those words across about 2.5 to 3 times as many turns, which suits bubbles. The flaw is that every scene is the same size whatever its job, and that 8 to 9 beats behaves as a target. The note suggests 4 to 6 for a routine or repeated act and 8 to 10 for a Listen or the capstone call, as judgement and not data. The Hades contrast is worth imitating: in Nyx's first scene, long turns run into Zagreus's "Yes. We are." and her "Then go." Sky's earlier sizing pick also differs: bubbles filling the act's own seconds, with Listen at 42 s and about 14 lines ([memory note](/Users/sky/.claude_account2/projects/-Users-sky-Projects-suborned/memory/dialogue-reveals.md)). That pick was for lines during the act and not a cutaway, so it does not settle this.

"Too short" and "the reading, it's too much" look less opposed than writer.md presents them. The second was his answer about the HUD ([memory note](/Users/sky/.claude_account2/projects/-Users-sky-Projects-suborned/memory/less-reading.md)), and the first was about conversations, so speech is the exception to less text. That is worth confirming with him. The budget holds on the note's estimates: the Help route (Listen, Help, Help, Call) is 288 words and 33 bubbles, about 66 to 108 seconds, and the Pressure route is 218 words and 25 bubbles, about 50 to 82 seconds. The range runs from silent reading to subtitle pace; the silent-reading rate comes from a search summary of [Brysbaert 2019](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf). The 33 presses may feel like load even at that word count, and no source measures it. Evidence on skipping is old but points one way: Mass Effect 2 saw 15% of conversations skipped, higher in hubs and much lower at the climax, in a fully voiced game ([GameBanshee](https://www.gamebanshee.com/news/99618-mass-effect-2-statistics-interview.html)). Ingold says "if you give people at least half an excuse not to read something then they won't" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/how-to-remove-the-frustration-of-reading-in-games)). So let the capstone run long, keep repeats short, and keep Skip free. The stopped clock is doing real work: an exploratory study of 64 players found 73 to 87% of subtitles were not fixated during manual play ([Lan et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12452717/)).

**The 3 s bubble in GDD §10 is too fast at the top of the range.** An average line of 46 characters at 3 s is 15 characters a second, which is comfortable. A full 12-word line of about 63 characters is 21, past Netflix's adult limit of 20 ([Netflix](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/217350977-English-USA-Timed-Text-Style-Guide)). The note's suggestion is to hold each bubble for characters divided by 15 to 17 per second, with a floor near 1 s and click to advance. Show the whole line at once, as Pentiment's outlined text does ([PCGamesN](https://www.pcgamesn.com/pentiment/obsidian-rpg-game-ux)). No study compares that with a typewriter reveal, so this is a preference with a reason.

Our documents conflict on staging. Writer.md says "People are at work while they talk. Plates, tables, the wheel, the clock", while [GDD §10](/Users/sky/Projects/suborned/GDD/the-suborned-agent-gdd-v0.33.0-2026-10-30.md) sets both people "to idle, facing, feet planted". A beat in scenes.json is only speaker and line, so a silence, a glance or a set-down plate cannot exist, and all subtext must be spoken. Mamet's silent-film test, Pinter's first silence, Hasson's turned back and Versu's moods ([Emily Short](https://emshort.blog/2017/05/18/mailbag-writing-for-versu/)) all need a wordless beat, and a wordless beat also cuts reading. Staging could replace lines: "He's looking again" could be a one-beat cut, and "People can see this door" could be her glance. Tactical Breach Wizards, the closest cousin, shows simple animation can carry this ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/let-s-compare-and-contrast-the-narrative-design-of-tactical-breach-wizards-and-into-the-breach)). The evidence on whether a short scene feels complete is craft advice only. The note's test: want against want, a fact arriving sideways, something that turns, an ending that changes the player's state, and a reason it stops. Ingold advises letting a person get back to work or grow irritated, so each talk has "its own in-built tension" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/designing-investigate-conversations)). A customer or the chime gives that exit without a quip.

## Most of the sameness is requested by our own inputs

One note could not check Fable's role card, so I read it, and it adds a cause. Under `runAgent` the card body is the system prompt, and [the card](/Users/sky/Projects/suborned/.claude/agents/dialogue-designer.md) says "bubbles ≤ 10 words", "text is a light layer" and "Conversation scenes reuse the scene lines: opening beats only". It also applies UI copy rules to speech: "concise... useful (what, why, fix)". So the model hears 10 twice, in the card and in "aim for 10", and 12 once. That, with 36 lines at 9 or 10 and none at 12, is the strongest local evidence on the cap, though the note's point stands that no comparison run has tested it. Writer and editor share this card, so the editor brings the same habits to the same text. The card is generated, so fixing it means a GDD change and Sky's agreement.

External evidence supports the direction and rarely covers dialogue itself. Writers on Dramatron complained of "a bit too verbalised, there should be more subtext" ([Mirowski et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2209.14958)), and MFA writers said a model is "much better at telling than showing" ([Chakrabarty et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2309.12570)). Measured work finds targeted edits beat raw output and human edits beat both: preference ranks of about 1.5 for writer-edited, 1.99 for LLM-edited and 2.5 for raw, with lower better, on prose paragraphs ([Chakrabarty, Laban, Wu](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2409.14509)). Few-shot examples beat none, with little gain from 2 to 10 on everyday prose ([Wang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.14543v1)). Stories from one frontier model were judged similar 80 to 90% of the time against 30.8% for humans; temperature did little and "as different as possible" barely helped ([Thennal DK & Hatzel](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.17350)). Banning a word can prime it, in one study of one open model ([Rana](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.08070v1)), and vendor guidance says to state what to do and to use 3 to 5 diverse examples ([Anthropic](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)). A written bible alone looks insufficient: Ubisoft's NEO NPC had authored backstory and style and press excerpts still called the output "somewhat robotic", though that ran live ([Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/video-games/ubisoft-nvidia-and-inworld-ai-partnership-to-produce-neo-npc-game-characters-with-ai-backed-responses)). A passing check will not mean good writing. `check()` can count question-and-answer pairs and line spread, and it cannot judge whether a turn has a cause. Short's warning about "10,000 bowls of plain oatmeal" and her test, "whether the generation is connected to anything mechanical", point at what our generator already gets right: facts bound from sim data and scenes tied to `learned` and `opens` ([Emily Short](https://emshort.blog/2016/09/21/bowls-of-oatmeal-and-text-generation/)). Her field keeps the voice authored and lets the system select and combine. We currently ask a model to invent the voice, carry the exposition and supply the wit at once.

So the human step should change, with humans setting the voice first. Players preferred Disco Elysium's designers' lines to GPT-4 infills even with the game's own dialogue as context ([Akoury et al.](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-emnlp.151/)). Ubisoft's Ghostwriter confined generation to barks, with writers defining, picking and polishing ([Ubisoft](https://news.ubisoft.com/en-us/article/7Cm07zbBGy4Xml6WgYi25d/the-convergence-of-ai-and-creativity-introducing-ghostwriter)). The note's proposed order is: a human writes the voice samples and two complete scenes until Sky says "yes, that's them"; a model drafts options for the Handover's six scenes and a human writes the last version; generated missions then use those exemplars, sim facts and staged passes, with sampled human review. No studio has published a controlled comparison of this. Fine-tuning has the strongest result in the literature, on authors' whole bodies of work, and we have no such corpus ([Chakrabarty, Ginsburg, Dhillon](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.13939)).

## Where the notes disagree

**What the 12-word limit is doing.** The notes offer three readings. One says the cap is about right and "aim for 10" is the problem, since writers fill targets. Another blames compression: about 73 words must carry who they are, the stakes, the fact, the next step, a city detail and temperament, so subjects and articles fall away. The third says only that the cap allows 1 to 12 words and most of that range sits unused. The notes also split on the cure. One proposes a quota per scene, at least two lines of 4 words or fewer and one of 11 or 12. Another proposes per-speaker ranges and a per-scene budget. A third warns to vary by want, and Ingold warns against rigid rules like "all dialogue has to be five words long" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/how-to-remove-the-frustration-of-reading-in-games)). No study tests word caps on voice. My view is that the cap can stay as a UI limit, since 12 words is about 63 characters and fits two 40-character lines ([Game Accessibility Guidelines](https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/if-any-subtitles-captions-are-used-present-them-in-a-clear-easy-to-read-way/)). Drop the target, and prefer a band limit over a quota, because a quota invites the same mechanical filling. One comparison run would separate target-filling from compression.

**Individual lines.** The notes read the same lines in opposite ways, which is a reason to distrust any single audit, including this one.

| Line | Read as good | Read as a fault |
|---|---|---|
| "If the Office hears about Okafor, we're both finished." | Among the more natural lines: one thought in one sentence | Undoes her denial to brief the player; a stock phrase |
| "He crosses the whole square for one coffee, Inez." | Best in `listen-inez`: small, human, and it is the evidence | Name for the player's benefit; a retort; a continuity break |
| "Heavy. Spell it out. I've been paid vague before." | Could only be Zhou | Staccato; a sheet phrase spoken aloud |
| "Let me sound like myself first." | Worth keeping | A sheet direction spoken aloud |
| "I've had a good look at your face." | Strongest ending | A written closer |
| "Nothing's wrong." | The benchmark lie | Stock phrase |

**Repeating the fact.** One note says the Scout and the card say the same thing, so one may be enough. Another wants the Scout to carry the facts in place of confessions. Sky's words favour keeping more. **Hand-writing.** The evidence favours a human voice lead, while the project tiebreaker says "systems > scripts (nothing hand-authored)" ([CLAUDE.md](/Users/sky/Projects/suborned/CLAUDE.md)). **Causes.** The notes attribute the rhythm mostly to our inputs, while Wikipedia's unreviewed guide reports recent models favouring short sentences anyway ([Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)). No run here separates the two.

## What Sky must decide

1. **Does "clearly explain" bind the spoken lines, or the whole scene including the Scout and the cards?** Whole scene frees the lines to stay in character; spoken lines keep confessions like "we're both finished". I recommend whole scene. See [writer.md](/Users/sky/Projects/suborned/briefs/085/fable/writer.md) and [the samples](/Users/sky/Projects/suborned/briefs/085/conversations.html).
2. **Who writes the voices?** May a human write voice samples and the final Handover scenes, given "nothing hand-authored"? If so, is it Sky or a short paid commission? The notes have no cost figures. I recommend exemplars at minimum, with hand-final capstones.
3. **Which word limit stands?** The [GDD and card](/Users/sky/Projects/suborned/.claude/agents/dialogue-designer.md) say 10 and the contract says 12, a mismatch [parked on 2026-10-23](/Users/sky/.claude_account2/projects/-Users-sky-Projects-suborned/memory/bubble-word-limit.md). Should a rare 13 to 15-word line, or one turn over two bubbles, be allowed?
4. **Which format?** The full cutaway when the act lands (the [writer.md](/Users/sky/Projects/suborned/briefs/085/fable/writer.md) proposal), §10's 3 opening beats, or lines filling the act's seconds (his 42 s pick). This sets scene length and whether arriving partway matters.
5. **May people work during a scene?** This needs wordless beats, listener reactions and a small action set, which changes [§10](/Users/sky/Projects/suborned/GDD/the-suborned-agent-gdd-v0.33.0-2026-10-30.md)'s "idle, feet planted" and the `Beat` format, and costs Glyph and Forma animation work.
6. **Should bubble hold scale with length?** Replace "up to 3 s" with characters divided by 15 to 17 per second.
7. **What should the tone be?** Must the Fixer lose or pay in every scene? May comedy sit in scenes that end in leverage over a frightened waitress?
8. **Will he approve a small live test and read it blind?** Two scenes, current brief against exemplars plus stripped sheets, with no other change. A live model call needs his go-ahead, and his reading is the standard that counts.

One item goes to Motive and not Sky: whether Milena's coffee line breaks "never together unless she calls him".

## Conclusion

The brief's first round of fixes worked, and that is why the faults now look harder to name. The openings are late, the greetings are gone and the civilians no longer name their feelings, so what is left sits a layer down, in rhythm and in who wants what. A banned-tells list copied from the internet would miss it: the literal "not X, but Y" is almost absent here, while the 9-to-10-word wall sits in plain view, measurable by a few lines of code and traceable to instructions we wrote.

The sources carry real limits. Nearly all pipeline evidence is on prose, and many quotes passed through a summarising fetch tool, so recheck them against the originals before repeating them publicly; several are flagged as extracts or third-hand above, and the Kasavin and Korb [Hades talk](https://gdcvault.com/play/1026975/Breathing-Life-into-Greek-Myth), the likeliest source of short-line craft, went unread. No player-facing evidence shows these fixes change how a scene reads. The counts can show that the set is monotonous; only Sky reading a rewrite cold can show that the monotony was the problem.
