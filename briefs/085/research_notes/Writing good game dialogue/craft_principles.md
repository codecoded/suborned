## What do named game writers say separates good dialogue from bad?

### Takeaway
Few game writers have published line-level rules that I could reach. The usable ones say: a speaker must have a personal reason to say this, dialogue should behave like conflict or combat, the subtext carries the scene, and scripts must be tested aloud. The most line-level advice in games comes from Jon Ingold and from David Freeman, a screenwriting tutor who consults for game studios, not from RPG auteurs.

### Cited Findings
**Who is talking, and why them**
- **Josh Sawyer (Pentiment). One writer.** The test for handing a character information: "why is this character the character to tell the story?" and "if the answer is 'well, we need someone to tell it,' that's not a good answer." The information "has to mean something that's really important to the person telling it", and you should "communicate the connection of the story to the person, not just the story itself." He is talking about historical stories, but the test carries over directly. — [Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/pentiment-obsidian-josh-sawyer-interview-xbox-gamepass-1849932260)
- **Greg Kasavin (Hades). One writer, weak evidence.** A secondary account says each god, shade or gorgon has a clear personality and interests, expressed by commenting on the player's run. — [ScreenHub](https://www.screenhub.com.au/news-article/features/digital/jini-maxwell/hades-greg-kasavin-breaks-down-supergiants-unique-approach-to-narrative-262459) (extract)

**Dialogue as a contest**
- **Jon Ingold (inkle, Heaven's Vault), "Sparkling Dialogue", AdventureX 2018. One writer, widely reported.** He reads Blade Runner's owl scene as a contest: a cop tries to boss rich people around and they beat him. — [Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/1830798679) (extract)
  - "If all of the meaningful interaction is happening in the subtext... then the text itself doesn't matter that much." — same source (extract; the page returned 404 when fetched)
  - His bad version, as recorded by Robert Yang: "Hello. I'm Deckard." / "Hello, Deckard." / "You're a Blade Runner, aren't you?" / "That means your job is to hunt down replicants and kill them." — [Radiator Blog](https://www.blog.radiator.debacle.us/2018/11/notes-on-sparking-dialogue-great.html) (Yang says this is his interpretation, not a transcript)
  - His better version is built from three moves. Accept: "Deckard." Reject: "I'm here to see Tyrell." Deflect: "What do you do here, Rachel?" — same source
  - Yang's summary: "Your use of branching structure is a form of characterization." He also warns that "gamers have been trained specifically to ignore subtext." — same source
- **Chris Avellone (Fallout: New Vegas, Planescape: Torment). One writer.**
  - He praises "dialogue combat" and cites Dune, Garth Ennis's Constantine, Sherlock and Luther. He notes that voice-acted games are where "brevity and writing for an actor's tone is key". — [Scripts & Scribes](https://www.scriptsandscribes.com/2014/09/qa-with-chris-avellone/)
  - "I'm really exhausted of talking head conversations," and dialogue "should be able to be interrupted at any time." This is a remark about systems, but it also describes scenes. — [PCGamesN](https://www.pcgamesn.com/pillars-of-eternity/the-big-interview-chris-avellone-on-how-to-write-an-rpg)
  - "Always make sure dialogue doesn't get in the way for people who don't like it." — [RPG Codex, 2005](https://rpgcodex.net/article.php?id=4172)
- **Robert Kurvitz (Disco Elysium). One writer, third-hand.** A forum poster's summary of a GameSpot interview says dialogue is "written to be aggressive and personal" to hold attention. These are the poster's words, not Kurvitz's. — [RPG Codex](https://rpgcodex.net/article.php?id=11309)

**Liking people, and testing lines aloud**
- **Sean Vanaman (The Walking Dead, Firewatch). One writer.** "I try to endear people to the characters through their sense of humor." And: "You let the characters play off each other, and like each other, and respect each other, even if they don't agree." — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/writing-i-firewatch-i-and-capturing-the-beauty-of-being-alone)
- **Vanaman on casting.** He credits Cissy Jones with the range to convey meaning without it being directly in the dialogue. — [GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/?p=114178) (extract)
- **Obsidian's process.** Staff read scripts aloud, each playing a character they did not write, and noted where readers stumbled. The New Vegas DLC had 10,000 lines as "a hard maximum" and used silent characters who communicate by gesture to get around it. — [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2012-10-09-how-to-write-fallout-new-vegas-dlc.html)

**Overhearing as a form**
- **Thief (Looking Glass).** Sound "was also used to divulge narrative information, so that stealthy players could eavesdrop on NPC conversations" (Wikipedia, citing Jacek Wesolowski's 2009 Gamasutra piece). This comes from a critic and an encyclopedia, not the game's writers. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thief:_The_Dark_Project)

**Line-level moves: David Freeman, GDC 2008. One teacher's list, reported by Anthony Burch.** All from [Destructoid](https://destructoid.com/?p=22071).
- Avoid "A-B" dialogue: predictable setup and payoff, as in "How are you?" / "Good."
- Interrupt.
- Answer a question with a question.
- "Own track": a character ignores the other and pursues their own concern.
- Tangents, so characters aren't "expository robots".
- Drop the first word: "Going to the mall?"
- Use fragments.
- Delayed answer: a question answered much later in the talk.
- Leave a topic and return to it.
- Restart a sentence.
- Reply in a way that implies the answer.
- External interruption.
- Give bit players a personality even in one line.

### Inferences
- Sawyer's test is the sharpest one for Milena. Why is she the one who says this? At present the honest answer is "we need someone to tell it", and the scene gives her no stake of her own.
- Ingold's scene works because the cop loses. In the samples the Fixer loses nothing and is never out-talked. Only Zhou and Inez's closing line ("I've had a good look at your face") cost him anything.
- Yang's warning cuts both ways. Players skim for facts, so subtext that carries required information will be missed, which supports Sky's demand for clarity.

## How do strong writers handle exposition, and when is plain statement right?

### Takeaway
**Shared:** information should arrive as a move in someone's fight, or be repeated by a carrier the player trusts. It should never be the purpose of the scene. Plain statement is defensible when the player must act on the information. Disco Elysium's reported repetition suggests clarity and obliqueness can sit in different carriers. This fits Sky's "clearly explain" better than the pure subtext school does.

### Cited Findings
**Against information scenes (Shared)**
- **David Mamet, memo to the writers of The Unit, 2005.**
  - "ANY TIME TWO CHARACTERS ARE TALKING ABOUT A THIRD, THE SCENE IS A CROCK OF S***."
  - He also condemns any character saying to another "AS YOU KNOW".
  - "THE AUDIENCE WILL ONLY TUNE IN AND STAY TUNED TO WATCH DRAMA." — [SlashFilm](https://www.slashfilm.com/a-letter-from-david-mamet-to-the-writers-of-the-unit/)
- **Mamet, paraphrased from a copy of the memo.** The job is to make the audience wonder what happens next, not to explain what just happened. The balance of withholding and revealing is the writer's core task. — [Memo copy](https://gist.github.com/jm3/7882146)
- **Robert McKee, exposition as ammunition.** Characters already hold the knowledge the audience needs, so "at pivotal moments, let your characters use what they know as ammunition in their struggles to get what they want." The usual example is Vader's "I am your father". — [Medium essay on Dialogue, pp. 28-29](https://medium.com/@pirangy/exposition-as-ammunition-robert-mckee-dialogue-the-art-of-verbal-action-for-the-page-stage-and-e9b38a4d2361) (extract; the page returned 403)
- **Table dusting.** Two maids dust and chat to deliver background. A workshop report of McKee contrasts two lines, and both example lines are the blogger's, not McKee's. — [NOWW workshop blog](https://www.nowwwriters.ca/noww-blog/dialogue-shouldnt-say-it-out-loud)
  - Bad: "I'm so glad we've kept in touch all this time... What has it been? 20 years?"
  - Better: "You're the same immature ass that you were in high school."
- **Billy Wilder, via Cameron Crowe.** "A tip from Lubitsch: Let the audience add up two plus two. They'll love you forever." And: "be careful not to describe what the audience already sees. Add to what they're seeing." That second tip is about voice-overs. — [Open Culture](https://openculture.com/2013/08/10-tips-from-billy-wilder-on-how-to-write-a-good-screenplay.html). The wording varies between copies, and the idea predates the book: [Quote Investigator](https://quoteinvestigator.com/?p=11102).
- **Avellone.** "It's fine, and in fact, often more interesting not to explain everything at the outset." — [Scripts & Scribes](https://www.scriptsandscribes.com/2014/09/qa-with-chris-avellone/)

**For plain statement and repetition**
- **Disco Elysium.** The same forum summary as above reports "critical information repeated multiple times by the protagonist's various skills during key moments". Third-hand. — [RPG Codex](https://rpgcodex.net/article.php?id=11309)
- **Kevin Wong (a critic) names two faults.** "Captain Obvious", such as a dying character saying "I am dead". And "Tutorial Talk", characters explaining controls: "who is my instructor speaking to, exactly?" — [Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/7-different-types-of-horrible-video-game-dialogue-1724322643)
- **Guy Hasson (a blogger, low authority).** A character may name an emotion only if they actually feel a different one. — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/story-design-tips-10-dialogue-don-ts)

### Inferences
**The working split I would hand the writer**
- **Say plainly:** what is asked, where, when, at what price.
- **Keep oblique:** how people feel about it.
- **Why this resolves the conflict:** negotiation is the one situation where people really do spell things out. Zhou's "Spell it out. I've been paid vague before." forces the plain "Service bay, round the back, east yard." In that scene the plain statement is itself the conflict, so nothing is lost.

**The brief and the craft sources disagree**
- The brief says "Clarity beats subtlety." Wilder and Ingold pull the other way.
- Sky's instruction decides this for the game. Keep the clarity test, and apply it to the outcome: who, what was got, what next. Don't apply it to the emotion.

**Three carriers for the clarity burden**
- The Scout's closing line.
- The `learned` and `opens` cards, which are already the game's own voice.
- A negotiation line.

With those carrying the facts, the lines before them no longer need to confess. The sharpest example is "If the Office hears about Okafor, we're both finished." It follows Inez's own denial and undoes it just to brief the player. It is also a stock phrase. The Scout's line can carry that fact.

**Wilder's voice-over tip applied to the Scout**
- A summary should add to what was seen. "The door guard's her man" adds a link the player couldn't make. "Bureau debt" only restates.
- **Separate problem:** "Two ways in" appears to break the brief's hard limit that no line may imply a shown option is the only one, and the memory note that everyone can be turned more than one way. Flag it to Fable.

**Mamet's "two people talking about a third" cannot be obeyed outright here**
- A Listen scene is built on it, as overhearing is in Thief.
- The way to honour the rule is to let the third person be a weapon between the two talkers: Milena wants something from Inez, and Okafor is her leverage. Then the scene is about the two of them, and Okafor comes out sideways.

### Gaps
- I could not check the McKee book directly. Everything above comes through secondary accounts.
- I found no writer stating when plain statement is right as a general rule. The split above is my synthesis, not a sourced rule.

## What do screen and stage sources say on subtext, wants, scene shape, and entering late?

### Takeaway
**Shared and strongly stated:** every scene is someone wanting something now, against an obstacle, and it ends changed. A scene that only passes information is broken. Subtext is what the talk is covering up. Enter after the greetings and leave when the point is made.

### Cited Findings
**Wants and obstacles (Shared: Mamet, Sorkin, McKee)**
- **Aaron Sorkin.** "Somebody wants something, and something is standing in their way of getting it." — [Erik Bork, citing the MasterClass](https://www.flyingwrestler.com/?p=59982)
- **Sorkin.** "Intention and obstacle is everything." Without it you're "coming dangerously close to finger painting." The protagonist's tactics for getting past the obstacle become the core of the story. — [No Film School on his AMA](https://nofilmschool.com/2016/07/aaron-sorkin-ama)
- **Mamet's three questions.** "WHO WANTS WHAT? WHAT HAPPENS IF [THEY] DON'T GET IT? WHY NOW?" The character needs "a simple, straightforward, pressing need which impels him or her to show up in the scene." — [Memo copy](https://gist.github.com/jm3/7882146)
- **Mamet on scene shape.** A scene "must start because the hero HAS A PROBLEM" and "MUST CULMINATE WITH THE HERO FINDING HIM OR HERSELF EITHER THWARTED OR EDUCATED THAT ANOTHER WAY EXISTS." — [SlashFilm](https://www.slashfilm.com/a-letter-from-david-mamet-to-the-writers-of-the-unit/)

**Subtext (Shared: Pinter, McKee, Ingold, Lubitsch via Wilder, Hasson)**
- **Harold Pinter, "Writing for the Theatre", 1962.** All from [Wikipedia, citing Various Voices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristics_of_Harold_Pinter%27s_work).
  - "There are two silences. One when no word is spoken. The other when perhaps a torrent of language is being employed."
  - "This speech is speaking of a language locked beneath it."
  - Speech is "a violent, sly, anguished or mocking smoke screen" and "a constant stratagem to cover nakedness."
  - "I think that we communicate only too well, in our silence, in what is unsaid."
- **McKee on on-the-nose writing.** "It's bad writing. If you write what the scene is really about then you're in deep doo-doo." — [NOWW blog](https://www.nowwwriters.ca/noww-blog/dialogue-shouldnt-say-it-out-loud)
- **Hasson, Don't #3.** A character shouldn't announce their goals while pursuing them. A character chasing X while believing it is Y is better. — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/story-design-tips-10-dialogue-don-ts)

**Enter late, leave early (Shared)**
- **William Goldman's "get in late and leave early"**, with a similar rule credited to Mamet. Cut arrivals, departures, greetings and small talk: "As soon as the goal is achieved in the scene, get out." — [Karel Segers, The Story Department](https://www.thestorydepartment.com/screenwriting-in-late-out-early/). Scott Myers traces the mantra to Goldman: [Go Into The Story PDF](https://movieintheair.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/Screenwriting-Mantra-Enter-Late-Exit-Early-by-Scott-Myers-Jun-2022-Go-Into-The-Story.pdf) (extract). Others credit Syd Field, which those sources call shaky.
- **Wilder.** "The third act must build... and then — that's it. Don't hang around." — [Open Culture](https://openculture.com/2013/08/10-tips-from-billy-wilder-on-how-to-write-a-good-screenplay.html)
- **Cut exercise.** Take a scene from ten lines to five, to two, to zero, and ask whether its purpose survives. — probably [Script Mag](https://scriptmag.com/features/script-gods-must-die-writing-dialogue-cut-instinct) (extract; page matched by title, not confirmed)

**Action instead of words (Shared)**
- **Mamet.** Imagine a silent film. Log lines like "Bob and Sue discuss..." describe non-dramatic scenes. — [Memo copy](https://gist.github.com/jm3/7882146)
- **Hasson, Don'ts #4 and #8.** A wink, a sigh, a turned back. Words are a last resort, tried after physical action fails. — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/story-design-tips-10-dialogue-don-ts)

### Inferences
**Audit of the six samples, applying Mamet's questions and Sorkin's tactics (my reading)**

| Scene | Want vs obstacle | Does anything turn? | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| `call-inez` | Inez must lie to a man she loves, and he can tell | Yes: "You've never once asked me that" | The benchmark. Her "Nothing's wrong" is the only outright lie told in reply to a question in the set, and each line means two things to two listeners |
| `pay-driver` | Zhou wants precise terms and no knowledge | Partly: he sets a limit, and "Knowing costs extra" | Second best. Plain facts are themselves the fight |
| `help-inez-1` | Inez wants the debt gone and no strings | Yes: "Then take it back. No." | The turn is good. The Fixer's lines are only delivery |
| `listen-inez` | Inez hides things. Milena has no want of her own | No | Table dusting, with good touches ("Not where the tables can see") |
| `pressure-inez` | Fixer coerces, Inez wants it over | Barely. She folds in beat 4 | The Fixer narrates himself: "I don't enjoy this. It changes nothing." "We keep our word" |
| `help-inez-2` | Inez fears for Okafor | Missing. She goes from "what happens to him?" to "All right" with nothing changing her mind | Needs a reason for the yes, ideally something she extracts |

**Measured from the samples (my count)**
- All 8 lines that end on a question get an on-topic reply on the very next line. Only two of those replies are evasive: "Nobody who'd say" and "Nothing's wrong".
- This is Freeman's A-B pattern. Delayed answers, own-track replies, replying with a question and implied answers are the direct remedies.
- Work never interrupts anyone. The brief asks for "A line can break off because of the work", and in six scenes no line does.

**Sample rewrites (mine, each within the brief's limits, as illustrations only)**
- **Own track and delayed answer.** Current: Milena "How far behind are you?" / Inez "Three payments. I'll catch up. I always catch up." Try: Inez "Table six wants his bill." Then, two beats later, unprompted: "Three. Don't look at me like that."
- **Implied answer.** Current: Milena "He crosses the whole square for one coffee, Inez." followed by a confession. Try: Inez "He likes the coffee." Leave the fact to the Scout.
- **Menace by ignoring, in place of self-description.** Current: "I know. I don't enjoy this. It changes nothing." Try: "Table four's waving at you."
- **Reply implying the answer.** Current: "And then you never say his name again?" / "Never. We keep our word, whatever you think of us." Try: "Whose name?" The risk is that this reads as a stock quip, so judge it aloud.
- **A yes that is bought.** In `help-inez-2`, before "All right", Inez extracts something: "He walks back to that door. Say it." / "He walks back." Now the yes has a cause and the Fixer has given something up.

**What to keep**
- Openings are already late, which is the brief's request and Goldman's rule: "Tube came for you", "Official bay", "Keep smiling".
- Also keep "Let me sound like myself first", "You've never once asked me that", "Then take it back. No.", and "Who's looking? Nobody's looking." That last one is repetition that works because the intensity rises, which even Hasson's Don't #7 allows.

**A format problem the writer can't fix alone**
- A beat in `scenes.json` is `[speaker, text]`, so a scene cannot hold a silence, a gesture or a piece of work.
- Mamet's silent film, Pinter's first silence, Hasson's turned back and Obsidian's gesturing characters all need a wordless beat.
- A wordless beat also serves "the reading, it's too much". It is worth proposing to Fable and Glyph, who own the scene spec.

**The medium is harsher than the sources assume**
- McKee's complaint that on-the-nose writing leaves actors nothing to do, and Vanaman's credit to Cissy Jones, both rely on performers. Speech bubbles have no voice.
- Subtext here must come from a visible clash between the line and something the player already knows. So the fact has to be planted first, in the scene or just before it.
- "Nothing's wrong" works because the player knows otherwise.

### Gaps
- A search extract offered "enter with conflict and leave when there is an emotional change". The page I checked doesn't contain it and I could not trace it, so I am not attributing it.
- I found no direct Sorkin statement on exposition.
- I did not verify McKee's *Story* on beat changes, and I am not citing it for that.

## How do writers make a person sound like one particular person?

### Takeaway
This is the thinnest area of sourced line-level advice. The solid material is: listen to real speech, use what the character wants as the source of how they talk, test by reading aloud, and don't fake character with length or verbal tics. In the samples the problem is measurable, because everyone shares one cadence.

### Cited Findings
- **Elmore Leonard.** "If it sounds like writing, I rewrite it." — [TheWrap](https://thewrap.com/elmore-leonards-10-rules-writing-111911) (extract). "Try to leave out the part that readers tend to skip." — [Farnam Street](https://fs.blog/2013/09/elmore-leonard-10-rules-of-writing/) (extract). He also limits dialogue tags to "said". These rules come from a 2001 New York Times essay. **One writer, but much repeated.**
- **George V. Higgins, The Friends of Eddie Coyle.** Leonard called it "the best crime novel ever written" ([AbeBooks blurb](https://www.abebooks.co.uk/products/isbn/9781409127628), extract). Dennis Lehane puts dialogue at "a good eighty percent" of the book. Reviewer Mal Warwick says closer to all of it, and notes that the speech isn't always instantly clear, so readers follow it in context as in life. — [Mal Warwick](https://malwarwickonbooks.com/classic-boston-crime-novel/)
- **Freeman.** Bit players need distinct personalities even with one line. Tangents and "own track" reveal who someone is, because they show what the person would rather talk about. — [Destructoid](https://destructoid.com/?p=22071)
- **Hasson, Don't #5.** Don't characterise through length of speech. Making a nervous person talk at length is a weak shortcut. **Don't #6:** no "Boy, I sure talk a lot when I'm nervous." **Don't #1:** stop using names in lines, as in "Lisa, don't do that!" — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/story-design-tips-10-dialogue-don-ts)
- **Sorkin.** He reads his lines aloud to make sure actors can speak them. He doesn't commit to backstory until he has to, because a full biography can leave you "lost in the weeds." — [No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/2016/07/aaron-sorkin-ama)
- **Obsidian's table reads.** Someone other than the writer performs the lines. — [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2012-10-09-how-to-write-fallout-new-vegas-dlc.html)
- **Avellone on sourcing people.** "Everyone has a story, and it's a good one, they just may not realize it until you ask." — [Scripts & Scribes](https://www.scriptsandscribes.com/2014/09/qa-with-chris-avellone/)
- **Sawyer on connection.** A story should show its link to the teller, so what someone chooses to tell shows who they are. — [Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/pentiment-obsidian-josh-sawyer-interview-xbox-gamepass-1849932260)
- **Kurvitz.** "Aggressive and personal" (a third-hand summary). — [RPG Codex](https://rpgcodex.net/article.php?id=11309)

### Inferences
**The cadence is uniform, and I can measure it (my count, 50 lines)**
- Mean 8.7 words, standard deviation 1.7.
- By speaker: Fixer mean 9.1, range 6-10. Inez mean 8.9, range 6-10.
- A waitress in terror and a professional fixer talk in lines of the same length. Neither ever says fewer than 6 words.
- Nearly everyone also uses the same shape: a clipped fragment, then a short declarative. "Nobody. I went to the window and paid it." "Heavy. Spell it out." "Stay there. I'm coming."
- That reads as one noir writer, and I'd guess it is much of what Sky hears as "the whole speech is bad". This is untested.
- The brief's 12-word cap allows 1 to 12 words, so most of the range sits unused.

**Caution:** Freeman's fragments applied to everyone produce exactly this monotony, and Hasson warns against length as a shortcut. Vary by the person's want, not by a length quota.

**Voice sheet to give the writer (my synthesis from the above, not a sourced method).** For each named person, one line each on:
- What they notice first. Inez: who can see. Zhou: price and exposure. Okafor: anything out of routine, which is why "You've never once asked me that" works.
- What they refuse to say.
- What they talk about in place of the subject (own track). Inez: tables. Zhou: the bay.
- How they answer a question. Inez: the wrong question. Zhou: with a price. Fixer: a smaller version of the truth.

**The Fixer is the voice to fix first.** He has 18 of 50 lines and no temperament of his own, only function. Vanaman's and Ingold's points suggest letting him be liked or beaten somewhere: dry humour at his own expense, or a line he loses.

**Swap test (already in the brief), applied:** "What do you want? Just say what you want." and "Keep it. I'll bring the rest." could be anyone's. "Heavy. Spell it out. I've been paid vague before." could only be Zhou's.

**Use a read-aloud pass by someone other than the writer**, per Obsidian and Sorkin. It costs nothing and it is the only test here that is both cheap and widely practised.

### Gaps
- I found no reliable sourced advice on giving a character a limited vocabulary, or on what a character notices, despite looking for it. The "notice" point above is my own inference from good lines, and writers may well have said it somewhere.
- I could not source any claim about Higgins's or Leonard's line-level method beyond the above. Mamet's rhythm is commonly discussed, but I found no primary statement.
- Avellone reportedly kept an actor's audition playing while writing Ulysses. That came only from a search extract, and the Engadget page does not contain it, so it is unverified.

## What are the most common faults of weak game dialogue, as these writers name them?

### Takeaway
The same few faults recur: scenes that only pass information, saying the subtext aloud, predictable question-and-answer, characters who could be anyone, narrating the obvious to the player, and greeting-and-small-talk openings. Most of the named lists are from teachers, critics and bloggers rather than leading game writers, so the faults are well attested but the authority is uneven.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
**Which faults the samples show (my reading)**
- **Present:**
  - A-B throughout.
  - Uniform voice.
  - Table dusting in `listen-inez`.
  - Self-narration by the Fixer: "I don't enjoy this", "We keep our word, whatever you think of us".
  - Name for the player's benefit: "He crosses the whole square for one coffee, Inez."
  - A confession for the player: "If the Office hears about Okafor".
  - A turn without a cause in `help-inez-2`.
- **Mostly absent, and to the writer's credit:** greetings, intensifiers, named feelings from the civilians, and the openings the brief was written against. The samples already fix the faults listed in the brief. The poor reading now likely comes from a later layer of faults the brief names only loosely, chiefly A-B rhythm and one shared cadence.

**Suggested additions to `writer.md`, in priority order (mine)**
1. No more than half of questions get a straight answer on the next line.
2. Each scene answers Mamet's three questions in a note, and names what makes the yes happen.
3. The Fixer must lose or pay for something in every scene.
4. A secondary speaker must want something from the main one (Sawyer's test).
5. Don't let any two speakers share a sentence shape.
6. Allow lines of 1 to 3 words, and allow a wordless beat if the format changes.
7. Add a read-aloud pass by a second reader.

**A check script can only catch some of this.** `check()` can count question-answer pairs and line-length spread. It cannot judge whether a turn has a cause. Those limits are worth stating so a passing check isn't read as good writing.

### Gaps
- The Hasson and Wong lists are from a blogger and a critic. They are useful because they give concrete examples, but they carry less authority than the named writers.
- I found no survey or comparative evidence on which faults players actually notice, and none showing whether these fixes change a player's reading of a scene. Reading the rewrites with Sky remains the only real test.
