Rules architecture: a staged plan

Redline, brief 111, 12 Nov 2026. Your question: is the code built well for a rules system, and could adding and changing rules be easier, with tests worked out quickly and no risk to the game?

Short answer. The base is good: the engine is exact, runs outside Unity, and rejects anything it doesn't know. Three things make rule changes slow:

  • Every run's fingerprint includes every word of the rulebook. A note edit looks like a game change, and the recorded drive test was re-recorded 45 times in four weeks. 40 of those were for no change in play.
  • The game decides some rules a second time. The Unity side has its own copy of "can this order go now". Most of the Listen-and-Watch trouble came from there and from crew-made rules with no ruling behind them.
  • Some tests check clock ticks, not fairness. A faster act pace "breaks" 9 route scripts when no rule is wrong.

None of this needs a rewrite. Twelve steps, about 140 agent-hours in all. The first four take about 17 hours and pay off at once.

Recommended first step: 1 and 2 together (Splice on the tiers, Keel on the fingerprint), then 3 and 4.

Hours are agent-hours. Steps 7, 9 and 11 use AI at build time only. Nothing reaches the running game, and right and wrong are still judged by maths.

Where rules live today
  • In the rulebook (data): 547 numbers, all used, and 37 events with their conditions. Two-thirds of the file is notes and sources.
  • In engine code: 34 rulebook sections describe their rule in prose. The behaviour is hand-written to match (the takedown, sight, police, fights, traffic, the getaway). Only Watch, Listen and the four approaches are read straight from the data.
  • In the Unity game: its own "can this order go" for the ring, quiet order fixes (cancel what an agent is doing, nudge a move, queue a Watch), its own idea of what the cell knows (the door), and hints that suggest an order of play.
  • In the words file: which orders each refusal applies to, restated.
  • In the tests: 491 engine scenarios, 42 fixed-tick route scripts, 18 bot plans judged in bands, one pinned drive recording.

Brief 110 as an example. "Listen needs no Watch" was a one-line rulebook edit. The round still touched 32 files and left 8 Unity follow-ups (hint, "Next" suggestion, a waiting plate, proofs, words), plus re-plans if the speed dial moves. The "follow her while listening" part is new behaviour and would be engine code under any design.

Brief 105: five agents, about 114 files. Take car is new behaviour, so it stays code. Steps 5 and 10 would remove the Unity copy of its locks and the restated word lists.

The AI loop (GER) and the search database (RAG): where they help, and where they don't

The loop in tools/style-agent: one agent writes, another scores, code checks, the writer revises. Runs take about 2 minutes and 50–100k tokens, on the existing subscriptions.

  • Source check (steps 4 and 9). Code does the main work: no ruling, flag it. The AI adds "this conflicts with what Sky said on…", backed by a quote that code checks word for word. It catches rules in the rulebook. A rule hidden in Unity code is only caught once step 5 has moved it into the engine.
  • Exploit hunter (step 7). The bots may only read what a player sees on screen, and the plan format rejects anything else, so the AI can't cheat. Its findings go to Motive as evidence.
  • Rule-to-data drafts (step 11). Only after step 10, and only as a first draft for Keel.

The search database (SurrealDB, tools/rag) is the right tool for one job here: finding the rulings that bear on a change, by meaning, across the GDD, the decision log and the briefs. Cost for step 9:

  • Build: 5–7 h for three new sources (decision-log entries tagged Sky or crew; each rule with its source and reason; your quotes from the briefs).
  • Risk: none to the game. One cost: any rulebook edit changes the corpus, so the rulings go in their own database (or update piece by piece). Otherwise every rule commit re-indexes the whole GDD.
  • Wrong tool for: the rules themselves. They stay exact structured data the engine reads, never searched or paraphrased. It is also the wrong tool for crew coding, which uses exact text search (rg) on file and line. And it never judges whether a rule is right.

Not for: auto-tuning numbers until the bands pass. That goes against "laws, not sums" and "don't tune to win one replay". Also not for scoring whether the game is correct, since a 10/10 from a model is not proof, and nothing at runtime.

What not to change
  • The exact, Unity-free engine: whole numbers, fixed ticks, the same result on every machine and in every language.
  • "Unknown = reject" when loading rules and plans. Stable refusal codes, with the words kept apart.
  • Every number with a source and a reason; no unused numbers.
  • Bots that see only what a player sees, and the hard-line vs band split in the mission checker.
  • The seed sweep (about 26 s for 18 plans × 100 seeds) and the split test runner.
  • No rewrite, no outside rules engine, no scripting language, no AI in the running game.
