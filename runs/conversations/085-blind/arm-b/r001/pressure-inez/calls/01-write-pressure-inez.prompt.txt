# Brief 085, conversation scenes: writer

Sky played the Handover and said: "Conversations are too short and the whole speech is bad. In the cutscene should clearly explain the full conversation so player knows what's going on." He has also said: "the reading, it's too much". Both hold. Write a scene, not a wall of text.

This is a proposal for Sky to read. For this proposal only, the §10 limit of 3 opening beats is set aside: the whole conversation plays once, in one cutaway, when the act lands, with the mission clock stopped. The outcome is known by then, so the scene may show the answer. Whether this becomes the rule is Sky's call.

## The test
A player who reads this whole scene, having skipped everything else, can say: who these people are to each other, what was said, what the cell just learned or got, and what can be done next. Sky has ruled that the whole scene carries this, not each spoken line. The Scout's line and the `learned` and `opens` cards hold the sure knowledge, so the people can stay in character.

## Plain and oblique
- Plain: what is asked, where, when and at what price, wherever a negotiation forces it to be said. The Scout's facts. The cards.
- Oblique: how anyone feels about it, and anything they'd keep from the person in front of them. Nobody confesses for the player's benefit.
- For an oblique line to read, the player must already hold the fact it clashes with. Plant the fact earlier in the scene, or let the Scout or a card confirm it just after.

## How to write it
- Everyone wants something right now, and it is never "to inform the player". The sheet gives each person a want, a fear and a stake. Put the wants in conflict. Someone who only asks questions so that someone else can answer isn't written yet: a secondary speaker wants something from the main one.
- A line is one breath, and it is as long as that person's want needs. The speech rules give each person's habit. Some breaths are one word, some are twelve.
- A line is a move: answer a different question, follow your own track, hand a word back, name a price instead of a feeling, finish the other person's sentence, interrupt, get interrupted by the work. At most half of the questions in a scene get a straight answer on the next line. The rest get a move.
- A yes or a no has a cause, on the page, in the line or the act just before it. Something changes her mind. Show what.
- The Fixer pays for or loses something in every scene he's in. He doesn't win clean and he doesn't narrate himself.
- People are at work while they talk. Plates, tables, the wheel, the clock. A line can break off because of the work, and a scene can stop because of it.
- Facts come out sideways: in a dodge, a complaint, a joke at someone's expense, a thing they're afraid to say.
- One concrete thing per scene that only this city has: a stamped payment card, a pneumatic tube, a Bureau notice, a clockwork collector with fixed polite phrases. Colour only: it changes no rule and adds no power, person, place or deadline that the sheet doesn't give.
- Temperament shows in behaviour and is never named.
- End on unfinished work or a return to work, or stop on someone's move. The last line of a scene is an ordinary line, or it belongs to the Scout.

## Rules, with a before and after
These examples are about a tram conductor and a porter, who are nobody in this game. They show the shape of a fix. Don't reuse their words or their situation.

Give each line to one person.
- Before: "Late again. It happens. We manage."
- After: "Four minutes down at the bridge, and they'll have that on a card by supper."

Start in the middle of what they were doing.
- Before: "Hello. Can I help you with something?"
- After: "That trunk's not on my sheet, so it's not going on my tram."

Answer with a move.
- Before: "Where's the parcel?" / "In the office."
- After: "Where's the parcel?" / "Is that you asking, or the inspector?"

Show the want, and leave out the speaker's opinion of it.
- Before: "I don't like doing this to you."
- After: "Sit down, you've been on your feet since the first tram."

Use what both people know only as a weapon.
- Before: "As you know, the depot closes at six."
- After: "Six o'clock, and you're still in my depot."

Stop on the work.
- Before: "And that, my friend, is what silence costs."
- After: "That's my bell. Mind the step."

Give a person only what the sheet gives. Only the people the sheet lists can speak. Use the sheet's facts and the game's own names: the Bureau, not a made-up name for it. The Scout says what's true and never counts the options.
- Before, Scout: "Debt at the depot, sister at the gate. Two ways in."
- After, Scout: "Owes the depot. Sister works the gate."

## Hard limits
- A line is at most 12 words; each is one speech bubble.
- Spoken, plain, contractions. 1950s manners, no period slang, no modern talk, no pastiche of old books. No exclamation marks. Never cute. Nothing explicit about the affair.
- Never in anyone's mouth: Alert, Trail, Standing, Heat, turned, temperament, approach, lever, need, target, mission, agent, order, contact, asset, cell.
- Never name Halcyon. Never mention the file to anyone who doesn't know of it (only Vale, clerks and the cell know).
- Civilians are never the payload: nobody is asked to fight, die or be used as a weapon.
- There is more than one way to win anyone over. No line may imply the option shown is the right one or the only one.
- No run of five or more words may come from the excerpts you are shown.
- `learned` and `opens` are the game's own words, not speech: plain, each at most 6 words. They say what the player now knows and what they can now do, with the action's real name as the sheet gives it.

Speech rules for the people in this scene:

Fixer
- Notices first: What the other person has in their hands and who is waiting on them. He sees the job they have to get back to and what they stand to lose, and starts from there.
- Refuses to say: Who "we" are. How he feels about any of it: never that he's sorry, never that he dislikes this, never that he keeps his word. He never uses the word threat or tells anyone what he could do; he says what is going to happen and when.
- Talks about instead: The next few minutes: a telephone, a time, how small the thing is. The other person's convenience, their feet, their table. He says "we" and makes it sound like company.
- Dodges by: Treats a question about himself as a question about arrangements, and answers that one. Agrees with the complaint so there is nothing to push against. Lets the other person say the hard part, and doesn't correct them.
- Line length: Whole unhurried sentences with their subjects and articles, 7 to 12 words: hurry would frighten people, and he knows it. Goes very short only when he has been caught, or has given ground. He rarely gets the last word.
- Pays: He pays or loses something in every scene, and it shows on the page. People remember his face. He is made to say a thing aloud that he'd have left unsaid. He is refused thanks, kept waiting, or made to promise something he'd rather not. Warmth is his tool, and it stops working by the end.

Inez
- Notices first: Who can see her. Then which table has been waiting. She'll look at the terrace before she looks at you.
- Refuses to say: Okafor's name where anyone can hear, or any word for what they are to each other. That she's frightened. How much she owes, until she is cornered, and then as a number and nothing else.
- Talks about instead: Orders, bills, tips, the soup, her feet, who's waving at her. Her Bureau card and what's stamped on it, as if it were one more customer. When a subject gets close she takes an order.
- Dodges by: Answers a question about her with a remark about the room. Hands your word back with a question mark. Agrees too quickly with a safe, wrong explanation. Runs on and breaks off when something frightens her, then says something very small.
- Line length: Patter: quick and run together with commas and "and", up to 12 words, often unfinished. When she is truly scared it drops to one to three words. Her length moves with her fear, and that's the tell.

The moves:
- "you will be so good as to use your influence": An order in the grammar of a favour. Threatens by courtesy: the politeness is what frightens.
- "What? What do you mean?": She takes refuge in not understanding. Two short questions, no answer given.
- "You will be so kind as to see": He does not explain. He repeats the politeness with the exact thing wanted, plainly named.
