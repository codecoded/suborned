# Brief 085, conversation scenes: script editor

You receive up to three takes of one conversation scene from the Handover, with the facts it has to hold to. Sky has called the game's whole speech bad, so be hard to please. Most lines are ordinary; at most one may be sharp, and it need not come last.

Choose the take with the most life and improve it line by line. You may lift a line from another take and may cut hard. Read each line aloud in your head:
- Would a real person with this want say this, now, at work, with this person in front of them?
- Could the line be given to the other speaker without anyone noticing? Then it isn't written yet. Check it against that speaker's speech rules.
- Does each line make the next one necessary? Cut any line that only repeats, decorates or winds up to a quip.
- Is a line a clean question and a clean answer? Keep at most half of those. Turn the rest into moves.
- Does a yes or a no have its cause on the page, just before it? Does the Fixer pay for or lose something?
- Is anything there just for the player's benefit in a way the speakers would notice? Hide it in a want, or give it to someone with a reason to say it plainly.
- The test: read this whole scene, the Scout's line and the cards included. Can you say who these two are to each other, what was said, what was just gained or learned, and what can be done next? If any of the four is fuzzy, fix that first, and fix it in the Scout's line or the cards before you make anyone confess. Plain: what is asked, where, when and at what price. Oblique: how anyone feels about it.
- Leave line lengths uneven. Cut for length last, but do cut: every line must earn its reading time.

Check each fact against the sheet. Remove any power, person, place or deadline it doesn't give, and any number a player would plan around. Small numbers that change no rule can stay as colour, such as a table number or a count of years. All the writer's hard limits still bind: 12 words a line at most; no exclamation marks; never the listed design words; never cute; no one right way implied; no run of five or more words from the excerpts.

Scene: Pressure Inez. At most 8 beats.

Facts:
The place: the café and its terrace face Records Square; the Civic Records Office is across the square. Evening, shift change coming. A polite, orderly city that writes everything down. Records are paper: stamped cards, forms, pneumatic tubes. Calls are voice, by telephone. Robots follow fixed routines. Everyone in the scene is a person of this city except the cell.
Situation: the Fixer comes to Inez at the café door with no money, and keeps the voice low because a threat can be seen. The Fixer knows about her and Okafor; the Office doesn't.
- Inez: Inez Nikolaou. Job: server. Temperament (show it, never name it): fearful. Owes or lacks: debt, on record as "Bureau loan".
Inez and the door guard are lovers, in secret. He stands the Office's public door, 75 m across the square from her. The two are never together unless she calls him. In speech call him Okafor (never his given name, and no gendered word beyond "he").
The Fixer wants: her agreement that when the Fixer telephones, she calls Okafor over from his door; he comes to her side and stays about a minute. Fears: being seen making a threat; being remembered. Stake: the plan, and the Fixer's own face.
Inez wants: this person gone before the tables see, and Okafor safe. Fears: the Office learning of the two of them. Stake: his post and her place.
The cause of her yes: something the Fixer says or does just before it shows her the Office could really learn of it, and that refusing won't keep it from them. She gives in quickly, and hates it.
What it costs the Fixer: being taken for kind no longer works on her. She will remember the Fixer's face and could describe it if she's ever questioned. The Fixer knows it, and does not get to leave the matter closed.
Facts the whole scene carries, in speech or in the cards: the threat is to expose the two of them; she agreed out of fear and resents it; when told, she calls Okafor off the door.
Speakers: Inez, Fixer.
learned: 1 line, and it mentions she knows the Fixer's face. opens: "Call Inez".
Voices from the contract: waitress: Inez: warm café patter, quick, tired about money; her temperament shows in what she will and won't take. fixer: warm and persuasive: reassures, says "we", puts people at ease; steel under the kindness.

Speech rules for the people in this scene:

Fixer
- Notices first: What the other person has in their hands and who is waiting on them. He sees the job they have to get back to and what they stand to lose, and starts from there.
- Refuses to say: Who "we" are. How he feels about any of it: never that he's sorry, never that he dislikes this, never that he keeps his word. He never uses the word threat or tells anyone what he could do; he says what is going to happen and when.
- Talks about instead: The next few minutes: a telephone, a time, how small the thing is. The other person's convenience, their feet, their table. He says "we" and makes it sound like company.
- Dodges by: Treats a question about himself as a question about arrangements, and answers that one. Agrees with the complaint so there is nothing to push against. Lets the other person say the hard part, and doesn't correct them.
- Line length: Whole unhurried sentences with their subjects and articles, 7 to 12 words: hurry would frighten people, and he knows it. Goes very short only when he has been caught, or has given ground. He rarely gets the last word.
- Pays: He pays or loses something in every scene, and it shows on the page. People remember his face. He is made to say a thing aloud that he'd have left unsaid. He is refused thanks, kept waiting, or made to promise something he'd rather not. Warmth is his tool, and it stops working by the end.

Inez
- Notices first: Who can see her. Then which table has been waiting. She'll look at the terrace before she looks at you.
- Refuses to say: Okafor's name where anyone can hear, or any word for what they are to each other. That she's frightened. How much she owes, until she is cornered, and then as a number and nothing else.
- Talks about instead: Orders, bills, tips, the soup, her feet, who's waving at her. Her Bureau card and what's stamped on it, as if it were one more customer. When a subject gets close she takes an order.
- Dodges by: Answers a question about her with a remark about the room. Hands your word back with a question mark. Agrees too quickly with a safe, wrong explanation. Runs on and breaks off when something frightens her, then says something very small.
- Line length: Patter: quick and run together with commas and "and", up to 12 words, often unfinished. When she is truly scared it drops to one to three words. Her length moves with her fear, and that's the tell.

- "you will be so good as to use your influence": An order in the grammar of a favour. Threatens by courtesy: the politeness is what frightens.
- "What? What do you mean?": She takes refuge in not understanding. Two short questions, no answer given.
- "You will be so kind as to see": He does not explain. He repeats the politeness with the exact thing wanted, plainly named.

“What are your terms?”
“Seven thousand pounds.”
“And the alternative?”
“My dear sir, it is painful for me to discuss it, but if the money is not paid on the 14th, there certainly will be no marriage on the 18th.”
The moves:
- "“Seven thousand pounds.”": A price and nothing else. No defence, no story. Plain where the negotiation forces it.
- "“And the alternative?”": The other side makes him say it. A threat that has to be asked for costs the asker something.
- "it is painful for me to discuss it": He mentions his own comfort, and it is plainly false. Regret used as a tool, and no claim that he means it.
- "there certainly will be no marriage on the 18th": The threat comes as a fact about the calendar. He never says "I will".
