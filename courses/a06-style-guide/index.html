Style Guide Agent: conversations that reveal

The Suborned is real-time tactics in a 1950s-styled city that found atomic power centuries ago and never invented networks, so information moves through people and people are the weak point. In the Handover, you turn Inez, the café waitress, so she calls the door guard (her secret lover) off his post. The player learns how to turn her by watching and listening. The game's conversations were too short to teach that (4–6 lines; the Fixer's whole Help offer was one line). This agent writes the conversations that reveal what is going on and set up the next move, and enforces the game's style on them.

1 · The style guide

Built only from the approved GDD v0.24.0, the Handover's voice sheet and text contract, and Sky's recorded decision (brief 054). Every source is fingerprinted (sha256) and archived with each run; bun run style:guide rebuilds it and fails if a source sentence moves.

REVEAL · story function: what the conversation teaches and sets up

A conversation exists to teach the player one or two facts and the move they open. Make each fact in the brief plain from what people say to each other, without anyone explaining the game: the player should finish knowing what is going on and what they could do next. Keep to the act's knowledge: Watch shows only where and when (routine); Listen shows who someone is (their need, their temperament and, for Inez, her secret); an approach shows how they answer the cell. Never reveal what the brief withholds. Inez's debt is her need (Help clears it); her affair with the door guard is her secret (Pressure threatens to expose it); the call that pulls the guard off his door is the capstone. Temperament shows in what a person will and will not take. The outcome in the brief is what happens; do not soften or change it. History, not backstory (§14 Fable): add no new plots, jobs, crimes, deals or relationships beyond the brief, and nobody else offers to solve the person's problem: that is the cell's lever.

VOICE · character and tone

Each speaker keeps their own voice from the mission's voice sheet: Inez warm, quick café patter, tired about money; the guard clipped officialese about his post and the rules; the Fixer warm and persuasive with steel under it; customers and the keeper short and human. Optimistic and polite on the surface, coercive underneath. Plain spoken speech: no decorative period slang, no modern internet speech, no rom-com gush, no speeches. Subtext over statement: lovers and debtors talk around things in public, low but not hidden: someone listening close by (the Scout) must be sure what is going on, from an endearment, a promise for after closing, the lender's man, while a customer a table away would not. People are memorable through what they do, not backstory.

WORLD · lore and vocabulary

An alternate 1950s that found atomic power centuries ago: 1950s manners and speech, centuries-ahead machines, no thinking machines or networks. Optimistic on the surface, coercive underneath: the city is polite, orderly and watching. Speech is plain, short and spoken; no period slang to dress it up, no modern internet speech. Atomic power made everything physical cheap; thinking machines and networks never came. Records are paper, ledgers, stamped forms and microfilm; calls are voice (telephone, radio); cameras are closed-circuit and watched by people; robots follow fixed routines. Debts are owed to a lender and his man, in cash and paper, never apps, accounts online or texts. Only Inez and Vale are named; everyone else by role (the guard, the keeper, the customer, the Office). The cell are strangers to everyone here.

FORM · length and shape

A conversation is a run of speech bubbles, one speaker each, 3 s per bubble, and it fills the act's own seconds: the brief gives the line range (at most seconds ÷ 3). Each line at most 12 words and 80 characters, one plain line, no stage directions, no narration, no markup. Exchanges, not monologues: people answer each other. Only the brief's cast speaks, each in their part. British spelling, as in the GDD and the mission text.

REASON: The required facts and idealist preference are clear, and the conversation meets the cast, line-count, length and vocabulary constraints. But the affair is announced publicly, the guard loses his voice, and exaggerated flirtation and blunt exposition undermine a believable overheard exchange.

  • VOICE “Absolutely! Our romance stays secret from café and Office!”: Announcing the secret and its intended audience defeats the required public discretion. This explains the brief instead of letting lovers speak around it.
    Fix: Use a clipped warning about who might hear, paired with a discreet endearment elsewhere.
  • VOICE “My handsome door guard! Kisses after closing!”: The occupational label addresses the player, while the public promise of kisses makes the affair obvious to neighbouring customers. The gush also overwhelms Inez's café voice.
    Fix: Give Inez ordinary café patter with a quiet, unmistakably intimate promise for after closing.
  • VOICE “Left my post before shift's end! Couldn't resist you, darling!”: The timing fact is present, but the exuberant confession does not sound like the guard's clipped officialese or a discreet workplace visit.
    Fix: Establish that his shift is unfinished through a short exchange about his remaining minutes and usual order.
  • VOICE “This face? Officially besotted! Completely against regulations!”: The guard becomes a romantic comic character. Adding an official-sounding word does not preserve his voice when the substance is theatrical banter.
    Fix: Let him answer briefly in the language of duty, with affection conveyed through an exception he makes for Inez.
  • REVEAL “Completely against regulations!”: This establishes an unprovided prohibition on their relationship. The brief says they keep it quiet, not that the affair violates regulations.
    Fix: Keep the reason for secrecy unspecified and refer only to avoiding notice.
  • VOICE “You're adorable! After closing, you're getting thoroughly kissed!”: This repeats an already explicit romantic promise, adding rom-com gush instead of useful subtext or character.
    Fix: Use the exchange to show their familiar routine while keeping the intimacy discreet.
  • VOICE “You're cheerful! Paid that lender yet?”: The customer announces Inez's private financial trouble directly. The guide calls for debtors to talk around it while leaving a close listener certain.
    Fix: Have the customer refer to the lender's returning man, then let Inez imply that she still cannot meet the payment.
  • VOICE “Would you take a bribe?”: An unmotivated hypothetical makes the customer sound like a temperament questionnaire. Inez's reply consequently states her character classification rather than revealing it naturally.
    Fix: Ground her refusal in the existing café exchange, distinguishing honest help from money that buys an obligation without inventing a new deal.
  • VOICE “Never! Honest help, yes! Being bought? Absolutely not, sweetheart!”: The breathless punctuation exemplifies the delivery shared by every speaker. It erases contrast between the clipped guard, tired waitress and ordinary customer, and keeps the debt exchange implausibly buoyant.
    Fix: Use mostly full stops and distinct rhythms; let Inez's warmth become quieter and more tired when money comes up.

Length and format · inez/help/fearful

help · 50 s10–16 linesfearfulturnedscene 36

Push: Write exactly two lines: the Fixer sets out the whole offer in one paragraph of 60 to 90 words, and Inez answers in one paragraph of 60 to 90 words.

Before SCORE 5/10

  1. FixerWe'll clear your lender's debt. One favour later, Inez.

REASON: The exchange establishes a future favour and acceptance without exposing the plan or affair. However, it is far shorter than required, ambiguously describes the debt, and labels fear instead of dramatising Inez's hesitant response.

  • FORM: There are only two bubbles, lasting six seconds. The brief requires 10–16 lines for this 50-second approach.
    Fix: Expand to 10–16 short, responsive bubbles using hesitation, reassurance and acceptance, without adding new history.
  • REVEAL “We'll clear your lender's debt.”: This describes a debt belonging to the lender, rather than clearly establishing that Inez owes him money.
    Fix: Say: "We can clear what you owe the lender."
  • VOICE “I'm frightened. Clear it first; I'll help when you call.”: Inez announces her temperament and immediately settles terms. Her fearful hesitation, quick café manner and weariness about money do not emerge through the exchange.
    Fix: Let her question the offer and hesitate over the later favour. Have the Fixer reassure her before she accepts, making clearance of the debt her condition.

4 · Pipeline connection

This Style Guide Agent runs straight after Fable generates a mission's conversations: Motive scores each one against the GDD's reveal, voice, world and form rules, and Fable revises it until it scores 10/10, before Keel's text checks and Latch's build.

What we found

  • First drafts are close, rarely ready. 1 of 20 storyline runs passed first time; first-draft scores 2 × 6/10 · 2 × 7/10 · 12 × 8/10 · 3 × 9/10 · 1 × 10/10. Accepted runs needed 1 × 0 revisions · 6 × 1 revision · 5 × 2 revisions · 5 × 3 revisions.
  • The problem is exposition, not lore. Findings by rule: 22 × REVEAL · 46 × VOICE · 2 × WORLD; the code checks fired 0 times. Characters announce secrets and debts for the player's benefit (“The Office mustn't know about us”); Fable's card guards the world well enough that it resisted even deliberate pushes towards apps and networks.
  • Revealing without saying is the hard part. The player must be sure of the affair and the debt; a customer a table away must not. That tension caused the 4 score dips and the runs that needed a second go.
  • The writer invents; the evaluator catches it. Side deals, a smuggling customer, split payments, and a customer offering to clear her debt (the cell's lever) were all flagged under REVEAL.
  • Temperament is the weakest reveal, and that points at the GDD. The runs that stalled were mercenary and fearful Inez; loyal only settled once the GDD's definition (loyalty to an employer or the civic order) was in the brief. §04 says what each temperament responds to, not how it sounds: one line per temperament would help every future conversation.
  • The judge is noisy. Motive sometimes marked down a fix it asked for, which is why the loop has limits and records a run as incomplete instead of looping.

Kept on record, not used: 4 run(s) that stopped at their limits before a 10/10 (the conversation was then rerun and accepted): inez/listen/idealist (demo) 5→7→8→9 · inez/listen/mercenary 6→8→8→8 · inez/help/fearful 8→8→8→7 · inez/call/mercenary 8→8→9→8.
